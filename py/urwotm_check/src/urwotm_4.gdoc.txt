This
article
is
about:
the
accent
atnaḥ
hafukh
(AH)
the
accent
BHS
conflates
AH
with,
galgal
(Galgal
is
also
known
as
yeraḥ
ben
yomo).
Here’s
the
basic
story:
The
BHS
table
of
accents
lists
no
accent
called
atnaḥ
hafukh
(“inverted
atnaḥ”).
Yet,
BHS
often
uses
a
mark
shaped
like
an
inverted
atnaḥ,
i.e.
a
V-shaped
mark.
In
fact,
it
uses
it
too
often.
BHS
uses
a
V-shaped
mark
where
such
a
shape
appears
in
L
(the
Leningrad
Codex).
So
far
so
good.
But
BHS
also
uses
that
same
V-shaped
mark
where
a
U-shaped
mark
occurs
in
L.
This
U-shaped
mark
is
known
by
two
names:
galgal
(“wheel”)
(a
weird
name,
given
its
shape)
yeraḥ
ben
yomo
(“moon
of
a
day
[in
age]”)
(a
sensible
name,
given
its
shape)
The
BHS
table
of
accents
lists
an
accent
called
“galgal
vel
jèraḥ
[ben
yomo]”
but
associates
it
with
a
V-shaped
mark.
So
we
can
see
that
atnaḥ
hafukh
and
galgal
are
conflated
by
BHS.
In
BHS:
Atnaḥ
hafukh
exists
in
shape
but
not
in
name.
Galgal
exists
in
name
but
not
in
shape.
The
BHS
editors
were
not
the
first
people
to
conflate
these
accents.
Indeed,
this
conflation
precedes
the
era
of
printing!
But,
due
to
its
great
popularity,
BHS
is
primarily
to
blame
for
this
conflation’s
persistence
down
to
the
present
time.
Thankfully,
this
conflation
is
not
universal.
As
other
articles
in
this
series
have
shown,
there
is
a
sphere
of
publication
and
scholarship,
mostly
Israeli,
that
has
escaped
the
great
gravitational
pull
of
BHS
and
WLC.
In
that
sphere,
there
is
no
such
conflation.
Let’s
look
at
the
first
few
words
of
Psalm
5:10
in
“MAM
with
doc”
(Mwd),
an
edition
that
takes
more
care
with
accents
than
BHS
does
(color
added):
We
see
that
in
Mwd,
the
words
אין
and
קרבם
have
different
accents.
The
accent
on
אין
is
galgal
and
the
accent
on
קרבם
is
atnaḥ
hafukh.
Now
let’s
verify
that
Mwd
corresponds
to
three
great
manuscripts.
Here
are
the
first
few
words
of
Psalm
5:10
in
Ms
א,
Ms
ל,
and
Ms
ש1
(aka
שׂ
(sin))
(aka
Sassoon
1053).
(Thanks
to
tanach.us,
Sefaria,
Bar-Ilan
U.,
and
IHBMR
for
hosting
and/or
indexing
these
images.)
We
see
that
Mwd
corresponds
to
the
manuscripts,
modulo
the
shape
of
atnaḥ
hafukh.
In
Mwd,
which
uses
the
Taamey
D
font,
atnaḥ
hafukh
is
what
we
might
call
a
tuning-fork-shaped
mark
as
opposed
to
the
V-shaped
mark
we
find
in
the
manuscripts.
This
is
like
the
well-known
difference
in
the
shape
of
atnaḥ
between
some
printing
traditions
and
the
manuscript
tradition.
Now
let’s
see
what
these
accents
look
like
in
BHS:
We
see
that
in
BHS,
the
words
אין
and
קרבם
have
accents
with
the
same
shape:
an
inverted
atnaḥ.
The
BHS
table
of
accents
identifies
this
inverted
atnaḥ
shape
as
“galgal
vel
jèraḥ
[ben
yomo].”
According
to
modern
scholarship,
this
is
wrong.
Only
the
first
of
these
two
accents,
the
accent
on
אין,
is
galgal.
In
the
poetic
books:
Galgal
is
a
conjunctive
accent
that
serves
pazer
only.
Galgal
does
not
serve
oleh
veyored
or
any
other
accent.
In
the
21
books:
Galgal
is
a
conjunctive
accent
that
serves
pazer
gadol
(aka
qarney
parah).
Galgal
does
not
serve
any
other
accent.
The
second
of
these
two
accents
is
what
modern
scholarship
calls
atnaḥ
hafukh.
AH
is
a
conjunctive
accent
that
serves
oleh
veyored
only.
AH
does
not
serve
pazer
or
any
other
accent.
AH
appears
only
in
the
poetic
books.
Perhaps
we
should
think
of
pre-modern
scholarship
on
this
topic
as
as
merely
imprecise,
not
wrong.
In
that
light,
I
suggest
that
when
pre-modern
scholarship
refers
to
galgal
or
yeraḥ
ben
yomo,
we
should
read
this
as
“galfukh,”
a
portmanteau
created
from
galgal
and
atnaḥ
hafukh.
“Galfukh”
is
a
kind
of
perpetual
qere
for
pre-modern
scholarship
on
the
Hebrew
Bible.
Once
“galfukh”
is
substituted
in,
most
of
what
pre-modern
scholarship
says
about
galgal
is
not
wrong.
It
becomes
merely
imprecise
rather
than
wrong.
For
instance,
when
Wickes
says,
on
page
57
of
his
treaties
on
the
poetic
accents:
The
servus
of
Olév’yored
is
[...]
Galgal.
We
should
read
this
as:
The
servus
of
Olév’yored
is
[...]
Galfukh.
Similarly,
when
Wickes
says,
on
page
88:
When
there
is
one
[servus
of
Pazer,]
it
is
Galgal
Although
that
is
not
wrong
even
according
to
modern
scholarship,
we
should
read
it
as:
When
there
is
one
[servus
of
Pazer]
it
is
Galfukh
Galgal
as
distinct
from
AH
is
not
a
valid
concept
in
pre-modern
scholarship,
so
we
should
never
read
Wickes
as
meaning
galgal
in
the
modern
sense
when
he
writes
“galgal.”
Similarly,
when
reading
the
BHS
table
of
accents
(both
the
prose
sub-table
and
the
poetic
sub-table):
We
should
read
“galgal
vel
jèraḥ
[ben
yomo]”
as
“galfukh
vel
galfukh.”
Since
“vel”
is
Latin
for
“or,”
we
algebraically
simplify
this
to
“galfukh,”
yielding:
The
conflation
of
AH
and
galgal
is
further
complicated
by
poor
font
support
for
galgal.
In
the
documentation
for
my
Taamey
D
font
I
write,
“BHS
is
so
influential
that
even
its
shortcomings
still
reverberate
through
the
Biblical
world,
showing
up
not
only
in
other
editions
of
the
Hebrew
Bible
but
also
in
fonts!”
To
be
fair
to
BHS,
another
important
contributor
to
the
poor
font
support
for
galgal
was
the
somewhat-botched
way
in
which
support
for
a
distinct
atnaḥ
hafukh
was
added
to
Unicode.
WLC
faithfully
transcribes
BHS
with
respect
to
these
issues.
WLC
uses
the
code
93
to
represent
BHS’s
galfukh
accent.
In
its
19
Oct
2021
release
(version
1.3),
UXLC
broke
away
from
its
WLC
roots
by
starting
to
distinguish
AH
from
galgal.
But
to
make
this
change
visible
in
UXLC’s
canonical
edition,
tanach.us
had
to
start
using
a
new
font.
Otherwise,
the
distinction
newly
made
in
the
underlying
Unicode
would
remain
invisible,
since
both
AH
and
galgal
would
continue
to
be
rendered
with
the
same
inverted
atnaḥ
shape.
That’s
how
hard
it
is
to
escape
the
“gravitational
pull”
of
BHS.
Even
if
you
try
to
distinguish
AH
from
galgal,
most
fonts
will
thwart
you!
With
that,
I
conclude
this
article.
