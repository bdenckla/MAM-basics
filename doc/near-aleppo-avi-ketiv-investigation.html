The chapter notes give positive evidence for the dot

Both chapter notes quote דָּנִֵּ֣אל and describe hiriq, tsere, and munaḥ beneath “the dotted nun”: האות נו"ן הדגושה. The disagreement therefore includes an explicit prose assertion, not merely a dot in one encoded string.

At 14:20, the note adds that munaḥ is pushed beneath alef and illustrates that placement as דָּנִֵּא֣ל. That separate placement qualification also retains nun dagesh. This investigation concerns the dagesh discrepancy; the conditional edit retains the approved placement of munaḥ on nun.

What would settle the edit

An omission from the shorter introduction list is a plausible explanation, but remains an inference. Because the chapter notes explicitly name nun dagesh, removing it has weaker support than the shin-dot and meteg additions. Both verses should be checked independently before either stored form is changed.

The qamats reading is doubtful in the source

The introduction labels the form ל-כתיב?!=יְבֻחָ֔ר and asks whether heth has qamats. MAM’s chapter note attributes the doubtful qamats to Breuer, then reports that inspection of an enlarged image favors pataḥ, despite blur beneath the horizontal stroke, perhaps a trace of erasure. The note says Dotan also reads pataḥ.

The analytical comparison above makes that reported preference visible. It does not assert a new manuscript reading from this investigation, and it does not establish an Aleppo form.

Three questions must be distinguished

  1. Vowel placement: current near-Aleppo keeps pataḥ on bet and qubuts on heth. The reported Leningrad arrangement has qubuts on the second written letter, bet, and the other vowel on the third written letter, heth.
  2. Vowel identity: the list’s qamats is doubtful; the chapter note favors pataḥ. Adopting the arrangement does not require adopting the doubtful qamats.
  3. Dagesh: current near-Aleppo retains the qere bet’s dagesh. The quoted Leningrad ketiv form has no bet dagesh. A change of vowel placement would not, by itself, decide whether that dot should be retained.

What would justify a near-Aleppo edit

Near-Aleppo aims for an inferred Masoretic consensus where Aleppo is missing. A reported Leningrad peculiarity is consequently a comparison, not an automatic replacement policy. The useful question is whether independent pointed-ketiv readings support the Leningrad arrangement over the current inferred arrangement.

The note’s agreeing clause names Sassoon 1053, the Cambridge manuscript, printed editions, Koren, Breuer, and Miqraot Gedolot Haketer alongside MAM’s qere presentation. That agreement does not, by itself, supply each manuscript’s pointed ketiv. A broader source comparison should establish the vowel positions and bet dagesh explicitly before an editorial choice is made.
