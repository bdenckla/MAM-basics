## Reading the translation

Comment timestamps retain the source’s **IDT** (Israel Daylight Time, UTC+03:00).
The source order is retained even where the timestamps are not chronological.
Reply relationships are stated explicitly instead of reproducing wikitext
indentation. User mentions are represented by their displayed names, and signature
links to user talk pages are omitted from the English text.

**Vocal shewa** translates שווא נע; **dagesh ḥazaq** translates דגש חזק, the dagesh
that indicates consonant doubling. In Dovi’s explanation, “visually emphasized”
translates מודגש, referring to the distinctive printed form rather than adding
another kind of dagesh. **MAM** abbreviates *Miqra al pi ha-Masora* (מקרא על פי המסורה).
The translation preserves the speakers’ claims and qualifications; it does not
independently assess the linguistic examples or Dovi’s account of Unicode policy.

## English translation

### Comment 1 by Yosef Ometz

**Posted:** 21:56 IDT, 30 September 2026. **Position:** opening post.

Following inspiring precedents, such as the outstanding achievement of @Ben Denckla,
who succeeded in getting Unicode to add support for vocal shewa, it is clear that
the door is open to substantial corrections and improvements in the digital
representation of language and tradition.

In my view, the natural next step is to make a formal request to Unicode to add
official support for a character representing **dagesh ḥazaq** as well. If Unicode
does add dagesh ḥazaq, perhaps we could use the infrastructure already available on
the AlHaTorah website, where dagesh ḥazaq already appears. As @Dovi wrote, AlHaTorah
provides a digital source for adding vocal shewa to Wikisource.

Although dagesh ḥazaq is hardly used in modern spoken Hebrew [see translator’s note
1], its grammatical and interpretive importance in the Bible is enormous. In many
cases, the absence or presence of the dagesh completely changes the meaning of the
word and its syntactic context.

For example:

- The form “ימים” ordinarily means the plural of הַיּוֹם (“the day”), as opposed to
  “ימים” with dagesh ḥazaq in the letter mem, the plural of הַיָּם (“the sea”).
- The form גנב can be a past-tense verb, as in “He stole the object,” as opposed to
  גנב as a noun with dagesh ḥazaq in the letter nun, as in “His occupation is thief.”

Adding this sign to Unicode would be another welcome contribution to the accurate
and faithful preservation of the biblical linguistic tradition in the digital
realm.

I do not understand Unicode and computers well enough to approach them myself, so
I am putting this proposal before Wikisource’s learned contributors.

### Comment 4 by Ben Adriel

**Posted:** 08:29 IDT, 1 October 2026. **Reply to:** comment 1.

Leaving the general discussion aside, in the particular examples @Yosef Ometz gave,
even without distinguishing a consonant with dagesh from a consonant without
dagesh, it is easy to notice the difference between the words because qamats and
pataḥ are distinguished. The plural of יום (“day”) is יָמִים, with qamats; the
plural of ים (“sea”) is יַמִּים, with pataḥ. The verb גנב is גָּנַב, with qamats
followed by pataḥ; the occupation (?) גנב is גַּנָּב, with pataḥ followed by qamats.

### Comment 5 by Yosef Ometz

**Posted:** 11:02 IDT, 1 October 2026. **Reply to:** comment 4.

@Ben Adriel, well said, but I am discussing **pronunciation**, rather than how to
tell the words apart. Even someone reading “ימים,” the plural of “ים” (“sea”), who
has no idea whether it is pointed with qamats or pataḥ, changes the meaning to the
plural of “יום” (“day”) if he does not pronounce the dagesh ḥazaq, even though he
understands perfectly from the context that the plural of “ים” is intended.
Nevertheless, you are right that there are better examples than the ones I gave.

### Comment 6 by Dovi

**Posted:** 06:39 IDT, 1 October 2026. **Position:** new top-level comment addressing
Yosef Ometz and Mo Yu Hu.

@Yosef Ometz, neither Ben Denckla nor anyone else has any intention of adding dagesh
ḥazaq to Unicode. Why? There is a simple reason: it is already there! As Mo Yu Hu
correctly wrote, the request to Unicode was for both a **visually emphasized vocal
shewa** and a **visually emphasized dagesh ḥazaq** together, and both have already
been accepted together. There is likewise a desire to add **dagesh ḥazaq** to MAM
as well.

@Mo Yu Hu, I agree with you: it would be good to have a separate character for
“mapiq,” distinct from “dagesh” or “visually emphasized dagesh ḥazaq.” The same is
true of gaʿya/meteg and “silluq”: they are the same Unicode character, but they are
not the same thing. Ben Denckla wanted such a distinction for the few words in the
Bible that have gaʿya **after** silluq, where automated software has difficulty
determining which syllable is stressed. Unfortunately, however, this apparently
cannot be done. The reason is what Yosef Ometz correctly wrote: there is no
orthographic, that is, visual, distinction between dagesh and mapiq, or between
gaʿya and silluq. That is precisely Unicode’s criterion: only if the two things are
**written** or **printed** differently will they have different characters. For
“vocal shewa” and “dagesh ḥazaq,” it was necessary to send them photographs of many
examples from printed books to prove that these really are printed differently.

### Comment 9 by Dovi

**Posted:** 12:38 IDT, 1 October 2026. **Position:** new top-level comment replying to
Mo Yu Hu and Yosef Ometz.

@Mo Yu Hu, limitations of this kind can indeed be overcome through markup. That is
what AlHaTorah did, for example, to distinguish vocal shewa and dagesh ḥazaq even
before they were recently added to Unicode. Various publishers of Pentateuch
editions and prayer books do the same thing. The disadvantage is that this makes
the marked-up text unreadable. The chapter editing pages in MAM contain quite a
bit of markup, but they can still be read and changed manually because **the vast
majority** of words have no markup at all. If every vocal shewa and dagesh ḥazaq
had markup, the entire text would become unreadable. The current level of markup
in the Bible on Wikisource, in the editing pages, is only “what is needed to
display the text correctly.” Features that require additional encoding, including
features that let other websites use the text and receive updates, are available
only on [GitHub](https://github.com/bdenckla/MAM-basics/tree/main/MAM-parsed). The
text on GitHub is updated to reflect corrections and changes to the biblical
pages on Wikisource. As for software for searching, grammatical analysis, and so
on, AlHaTorah has sophisticated tools, and its biblical text is imported from
here. I think Sefaria also has tools, and its biblical text likewise comes from
here.

@Yosef Ometz, thank you for your words — that really is the whole idea! It happens
both on Wikisource and elsewhere because of the open license. Some of the
refinements to the biblical text made on Wikisource originate in reports received
from users of AlHaTorah and Sefaria.

Happy holiday to you both.

## Translator’s notes

1. The opening post has an incomplete phrase, “המדוברת המודרנית ושאינה,” after
   describing modern spoken Hebrew. The translation gives the clear core of the
   clause without supplying a meaning for its unfinished ending.
2. In comment 3, the source has “מיחה” where the context appears to require “מילה”
   (“word”). The translation says “word”; the source capture retains “מיחה.”
3. The opening examples retain their original pointing or lack of pointing. The
   fuller pointed forms in comment 4 are Ben Adriel’s examples, not additions by
   the translator. The source’s linguistic claims are preserved as claims by the
   named speakers.
4. In comment 9, “markup” translates קידוד when Dovi is discussing wikitext editing
   and display. “Encoding” is retained where the discussion broadens to additional
   machine-readable information. “Before they call, I will answer” in comment 8
   translates the biblical quotation as it appears in the comment.
