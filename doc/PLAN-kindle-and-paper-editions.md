## What this plan does

It plans self-published editions of three works, each as a **Kindle edition**, an eBook sold
through Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP), and as a **printed edition**, made by a
**print-on-demand service**, a printer that prints and binds each copy when it is ordered:

1. **The Hebrew introduction:** Hebrew Wikisource's introduction to MAM, mirrored in
   `in/mam-ws-intro/`.
2. **The English introduction:** the English translation of that introduction, whose mirror and
   upkeep a sister plan covers (“The English introduction”, below).
3. **The MAM edition:** MAM's text in volumes whose boundaries need not match the books', with a
   Decalogue appendix and perhaps a song-form appendix.

It says what each edition is made from, how one generator in this repository writes all of them,
how the MAM edition's volumes divide the books, which typesetting toolchain each edition uses,
what the rights and platform policies require, and in what stages the work runs. It publishes,
buys and signs up for nothing: every such act is Ben's, stage by stage (Stage 7).

### MAM's size in print, with its documentation notes

Ben, 2026-11-22, during the planning: “Regarding page estimates, make page estimates assuming
MAM's doc-notes will be included. This will make most books of the Torah (and other
Aleppo-missing sections) much longer than without doc-notes”. So every estimate below includes
the notes, and the text alone is given only beside them.

**How it was measured,** on 2026-11-22, as a dated observation for planning rather than a
maintained measurement: Stage 1 measures real pages, and Stage 2's generator reports them for
every volume thereafter.

1. **The text** was measured by a read-only sub-agent at commit `45a975f7`, from
   `MAM-simple/json-vtrad-mam/`, with ketiv and qere both counted, as MAM-with-doc prints them,
   and the combined cantillation in the three passages that have two; a recount from
   `MAM-parsed/plus/` matched its letters and atoms in all 39 books. The whole text has 929
   chapters, 23,202 verses, 306,525 atoms, 263,941 chanted words, 1,201,806 letters and 2,838,309
   characters, of which 1,046,086 are points and 251,134 accents.
2. **The notes** were measured by the planning session from MAM-with-doc's rendered pages, as a
   reader of that edition sees them: each verse row's note cell, with its lemma and its parts,
   and each book's big-doc page. They come to 406,468 characters, 14 percent of the text's
   characters across the whole, but 26 percent in Genesis, 35 in Deuteronomy, 42 in Daniel, and
   from 31 to 71 in the Five Megillot, and from 3 to 9 percent in Joshua through Ezekiel.
3. **Two page models**, both with a 0.875-inch inside margin, KDP's minimum for 701 to 828
   pages, and a title page for each book:
   1. **Model (a):** 6 × 9 inches, one column, Taamey D at 13 points on 20: about 1,210 letters a
      page in prose, and about 680 in Psalms, Proverbs and Job, set two stichs to a line. Notes at
      9 points, 80 characters a line and 42 lines a page.
   2. **Model (b):** 7 × 10 inches, two columns, at 11 points on 16.5: about 2,270 letters a
      page in prose, and about 980 in those three books, a stich to a column's line. Notes at 8.5
      points, 50 characters a column's line and 104 column lines a page.
   3. **Two note layouts:** each lemma and each part beginning a new line, as MAM-with-doc sets
      them, which gives the estimate below; or the parts run on as an apparatus, which gives the
      smaller figure in parentheses.
   4. A setting as loose as Koren's one-volume Hebrew Tanakh, listed at 1,387 pages, about 865
      letters a page, takes about 1.24 times model (a)'s text pages; that listing comes from
      booksellers' pages whose sizes disagree, and nobody checked it against a specification.
4. **Pages with the notes:**

   | Part | Model (a) | Model (a), text alone | Model (b) |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Torah | 378 (318) | 259 | 198 |
   | Former Prophets | 258 (243) | 231 | 138 |
   | Latter Prophets | 282 (262) | 249 | 155 |
   | Psalms, Proverbs and Job | 256 (227) | 206 | 166 |
   | The Five Megillot | 61 (49) | 38 | 34 |
   | Daniel to Chronicles | 180 (158) | 140 | 95 |
   | The whole Tanakh | 1,414 (1,258) | 1,122 | 785 |

5. **The notes lengthen the books unevenly**, as Ben foresaw: in model (a), Genesis from 64 pages
   to 100, Exodus from 54 to 75, Deuteronomy from 48 to 78, Daniel from 21 to 39 and the Megillot
   from 38 to 61, while Joshua through Ezekiel grow by 7 to 19 percent.
6. **No book comes near a page limit.** The largest, Psalms, is about 157 pages in model (a)
   with its notes; Jeremiah, the largest prose book, about 80. So a page limit bounds only a
   volume that holds several books, and a split of one book across volumes, Ben's one-to-many,
   is a choice of volume size rather than a necessity.
7. **The appendices:** the Decalogue page with its transcluded text about 24 pages in model (a)
   and 11 in (b), since it shows each cantillation twice; the Decalogue's base page, which holds
   each once, about 8 and 4; the eight songs in song form about 24 and 9; their further forms
   about 34 and 16 (the text sub-agent's measurement).
8. **The Hebrew introduction:** its root page, summary, five chapters and appendices hold 102,242
   words, about 258 pages at 6 × 9 inches, in 11-point text with 9-point footnotes, or 134 at
   7 × 10. The two codex keys would add about 47 or 19 pages, and the Westminster typing page
   and the technical guides about 102 or 43, mostly tables.

### How printed Bibles divide into volumes

`UPenn` below is the University of Pennsylvania's catalog, a record at
`https://find.library.upenn.edu/catalog/<id>`, the one library catalog that allowed the
sub-agent in; the National Library of Israel's and others' refused automated access.

1. **Da'at Miqra,** Mossad HaRav Kook's commentary series, has 30 volumes of about 25 cm for 20
   titles, its first volumes, Joshua and Job, from 1970 and its last from 2003. The publisher's
   set page and its listing of the volumes sold singly give 30, as Hebrew Wikipedia does
   (https://mosadharavkook.com/tag/%d7%93%d7%a2%d7%aa-%d7%9e%d7%a7%d7%a8%d7%90/ and its
   `/page/2/`; https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%93%D7%A2%D7%AA_%D7%9E%D7%A7%D7%A8%D7%90):

   | Title | Volumes | Division |
   |---|---|---|
   | Genesis | 3 | 1–17, 18–36, 37–50, at the parashot Vayera and Vayeshev |
   | Exodus | 2 | 1–20, 21–40, at Mishpatim |
   | Leviticus | 2 | 1–15, 16–27, the second inferred from the first's title |
   | Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges | 1 each | |
   | Samuel | 2 | 1 Samuel, 2 Samuel |
   | Kings | 2 | 1 Kings 1:1–22:40, then 1 Kings 22:41 to the end of 2 Kings |
   | Isaiah | 2 | division not found |
   | Jeremiah, Ezekiel | 1 each | |
   | The Twelve | 2 | Hosea to Micah, Nahum to Malachi, inferred from the volumes' authors |
   | Psalms | 2 | 1–72, 73–150 |
   | Proverbs, Job | 1 each | |
   | The Five Megillot | 1 | all five |
   | Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah | 1 each | |
   | Chronicles | 2 | 1 Chronicles, 2 Chronicles |

   The volumes run to hundreds of pages each, commentary included: Genesis's three are 728, 680 and
   576 pages on the publisher's product pages, and the extents in UPenn's records are of the same
   order. So Ben's many-to-one is the Megillot's volume, and his one-to-many is every title in two
   or three volumes; Kings shows that a break may follow the page count rather than the book's own
   division.
2. **Miqra'ot Gedolot HaKeter** (Bar-Ilan University, 1992 to 2019) gives the 24 books in 21
   volumes: Genesis at Toledot (25:19), Exodus at Mishpatim, and Psalms at 73 are split, while
   Joshua with Judges, Daniel with Ezra–Nehemiah, and the Megillot each share a volume (UPenn
   9924616153503681; Hebrew Wikipedia's article on it).
3. **Text-only editions:** Breuer's for Mossad HaRav Kook (1977 to 1982) in three volumes, Torah,
   Nevi'im and Ketuvim, of 25 cm (UPenn 9916431383503681); the Jerusalem Crown (2000) in one, of
   874 and 33 pages at 23 cm (UPenn 9930785903503681); Koren's in one, in several sizes, its
   personal size 1,312 pages (a retailer's listing,
   https://www.judaicaplace.com/koren-tanach-1-volume-medium-size-hardcover/9789653010512/), with
   a limited edition of 1963 in three; the JPS Hebrew-English Tanakh (1999) in one of 2,040
   pages at 6 × 9 inches (https://jps.org/resources/tanakh-customer-guide/).
4. **The order of the books.** Printed editions put Ketuvim in the order Psalms, Proverbs, Job,
   the Megillot, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah and Chronicles; the Tiberian codices put Chronicles first.
   MAM's introduction commits MAM to the printed order (`in/mam-ws-intro/ch2.mediawiki`, its
   sections on the order of the books and on how this edition presents it), and the volumes keep
   it.
5. **What it means for MAM.** Da'at Miqra's division would give MAM, with its notes, 30 volumes of
   about 23 to 82 pages in model (a), 47 on average, Leviticus 16–27 under KDP's 24-page minimum;
   Genesis's three would be about 30, 42 and 28 pages. Its volumes are thick because of the
   commentary, and Miqra'ot Gedolot HaKeter's 21 would be little thicker. So the edition can take
   their principle, volumes that break where readers expect and that need not match the books,
   without taking their division.

### Print on demand

A Hebrew book opens from what an English reader calls its back, with the spine on the right of
the front cover. KDP's help pages show no update dates; the other services' dates are given
where they show one. Abbreviations: `KDP` for https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/, `IS` for
https://help.ingramspark.com/hc/en-us/articles/, `LU` for
https://help.lulu.com/en/support/solutions/articles/.

1. **KDP** binds a Hebrew paperback right to left: choosing Hebrew as the book's language does it,
   and a file whose reading direction does not match is rejected. The interior PDF runs in
   reading order from page 1. A Hebrew hardcover can only be bound left to right, and KDP's
   Expanded Distribution, its route to other stores, excludes Hebrew paperbacks and every
   hardcover, so a Hebrew KDP paperback sells on Amazon only (KDP GML3FXP998V8WS4U, G201953020,
   GQTT4W3T5AYK7L45). KDP's language list shows Hebrew with no restriction, which conflicts with
   its Hebrew page (KDP G200673300).
2. **IngramSpark** binds right to left, in paperback and in case-laminate, cloth and jacketed
   hardcovers, through a “Right-to-Left Content” option chosen when the title is set up, which
   only its support can remove; the interior PDF then runs in reverse page order, and it has
   right-to-left cover templates for each binding (its File Creation Guide, pages 15 and 28 to
   33, https://www.ingramspark.com/hubfs/downloads/file-creation-guide.pdf, modified
   2026-08-24; IS 40815017557261, updated 2026-11-18). Its network reaches more than 45,000
   retailers and libraries, and for a Hebrew title it advises giving the English title first in
   the metadata (IS 5341507452173;
   https://www.ingramspark.com/blog/global-discoverability-guidance). The spine stamping of its
   cloth hardcovers takes English letters only.
3. **Lulu** has no right-to-left setting: the PDF runs in reverse page order, the cover puts the
   front panel on the left, and Lulu's store preview then shows the back cover (LU 64000255590,
   modified 2026-06-08). Its retail distribution needs metadata in Latin letters (“Rights and the

### Kindle

`KPG` below is Amazon's Kindle Publishing Guidelines, version 2026.3
(https://kindlegen.s3.amazonaws.com/AmazonKindlePublishingGuidelines.pdf).

1. **KDP accepts Hebrew eBooks,** and lists Hebrew for paperbacks and hardcovers too; upload of
   a PDF is limited to nine Western European languages, Hebrew not among them
   (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200673300). Its Hebrew page asks for a `.doc`,
   `.docx` or EPUB manuscript, rejects a file whose reading direction differs from the one chosen
   when the title was set up, and says that Kindle for Web, the reader in a browser, has no
   right-to-left support, and that X-Ray, Word Wise and page numbers are unavailable
   (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GML3FXP998V8WS4U).
2. **The support is new.** In June 2023 KDP told an author that Kindle readers could not handle
   Hebrew characters (https://corp.unicode.org/pipermail/unicode/2023-June/010560.html); the
   changelog of calibre's KFX Output plugin says on 2025-10-12 that Amazon supports Hebrew from
   Kindle Previewer 3.98.0 (https://www.mobileread.com/forums/showthread.php?t=272407); an
   Israeli publisher announced Hebrew KDP eBooks on 2025-12-21
   (https://alefbooks.co.il/news/digital-books-in-hebrew-amazon/). No announcement by Amazon was
   found.
3. **The route is EPUB 3, then Kindle Previewer, then upload.** Kindle Create's list of eBook
   languages omits Hebrew (KPG, section 2.1.1), and Kindle Previewer opens a `.docx` only for
   English (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G202131170). Any error or warning that
   Previewer reports blocks publication (KPG, section 2.2.1). Previewer only simulates a
   tablet, a phone and an e-reader, and the KPF file it writes cannot be loaded onto a device;
   its user guide added Hebrew in its revision of 2026-11-05
   (https://driz46fl8b4jf.cloudfront.net/UserGuideKPR4_EN.pdf).
4. **Right to left.** The package document must give a page direction other than left to right
   (KPG, section 11.1). Enhanced Typesetting supports `dir="rtl"` and Hebrew (KPG, sections 18.1
   and 17.5), on e-readers from the sixth generation and Fire tablets from the third, and in the
   iOS, Android, PC and Mac apps (section 17.6).
5. **Nothing shows that Kindle positions Hebrew marks from an embedded font.** Kindle supports
   embedded OpenType and TrueType fonts but prefers its own, and on a KF8 device the reader can
   turn the publisher's fonts off (KPG, section 11.3.8); malformed markup makes the conversion drop
   the font files (section 11.3.9). No Amazon document mentions complex-script shaping, GPOS or
   mark positioning. The reports found all predate the support: in January 2022 a book embedding
   David Libre showed its points shifted off their letters while the same font installed on the
   device rendered correctly (https://www.mobileread.com/forums/showthread.php?p=4192270); a
   project that builds Kindle books from Sefaria's texts loads its fonts onto the device rather
   than embedding them (https://kindleseforim.paritcher.com/). Nothing found tests cantillation
   on any Kindle, and no Koren, Sefaria or ArtScroll Kindle edition was found.
6. **No fixed-layout way round it.** Print Replica, made by Kindle Create from a PDF, is closed to
   Hebrew, since both Kindle Create and PDF upload exclude it, and does not run on e-readers
   anyway (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GMUKXSTBCN6F2TGM; KPG, section 3). A
   fixed-layout EPUB must embed its fonts, so it needs the same unproven shaping; the one fallback
   that reaches every Kindle is fixed layout with the text drawn as images, which cannot be
   searched and makes large files.
7. **File size and royalty.** The 70% royalty deducts US$0.15 a megabyte delivered on Amazon.com,
   and since 2026-07-07 needs a list price between $2.99 and $12.99
   (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634500, updated 2026-04-21;
   https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200634560). A reflowable volume of a few megabytes
   costs cents to deliver; a volume of page images would cost more per sale than its price.
8. **Other stores.** Apple Books supports right-to-left page progression and embedded fonts but
   lists Hebrew among the languages it does not fully support, without saying what is missing
   (https://help.apple.com/itc/applebooksstoreformatting/en.lproj/static.html); Google Play
   Books accepts every language and supports embedded fonts in EPUB 3, though features vary by
   platform (https://support.google.com/books/partner/answer/3316879); whether Kobo takes Hebrew
   was not confirmed.
9. **So:** one EPUB 3, with `lang="he"`, `dir="rtl"`, a right-to-left page progression and Taamey D
   embedded, is the master of every eBook edition; and the Kindle edition of anything with
   cantillation waits until a test book of the hardest mark combinations reads correctly on real
   devices, an e-ink Kindle, a Fire tablet, the iOS and Android apps and the desktop app, with
   the publisher's font turned on and off. Kindle Previewer alone proves nothing about rendering.

### Typesetting toolchains

HarfBuzz's sources are cited at commit `276cf4a7` of https://github.com/harfbuzz/harfbuzz.

1. **HarfBuzz shapes nearly every path, and it sets the marks' order.** Before a font's lookups
   run, it sorts each run of marks, stably, by its own modified combining classes, so that
   canonically equivalent strings render alike (`src/hb-ot-shape-normalize.cc`, lines 36 to 75
   and 375 to 405). For Hebrew it permutes classes 10 to 26 into the order that the SBL Hebrew
   font's manual recommends, beginning shin dot, sin dot, dagesh, rafe and holam, and leaves the
   accents their classes, so that every accent follows every point (`src/hb-unicode.hh`, lines
   320 to 349). Its Hebrew shaper then swaps one pair: after a patah or qamats, a sheva or hiriq
   followed by U+05BD or an accent of class 220 (`src/hb-ot-shaper-hebrew.cc`, lines 165 to
   190), a rule that came from harfbuzz/harfbuzz#2947
   (https://github.com/harfbuzz/harfbuzz/issues/2947) and was first released in HarfBuzz 5.0.0
   (https://github.com/harfbuzz/harfbuzz/releases/tag/5.0.0). A combining
   grapheme joiner, of class 0, stops the sort, and Taamey D deletes it (`Taamey-D/README.md`,
   “Taamey D support scope”).
2. **So MAM's stored order reaches the font only across a joiner,** and any HarfBuzz path draws
   MAM-normal text as it draws the text's NFC form. This repository's fragility check models that
   reordering for HarfBuzz 15.0.0 (“Sources in this repository”, item 3), and Taamey D's shaping
   checks shape with HarfBuzz 15.0.0, which uharfbuzz 0.57.0 bundles in this environment (read on
   2026-11-22). A typesetter therefore draws MAM as those checks do when its HarfBuzz behaves as
   15.0.0's does; Stage 1 records each engine's version.
3. **Which engines use it.** Chromium's Blink shapes with HarfBuzz
   (https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/fonts/README.md),
   Pango has since 2019, and so WeasyPrint
   (https://blogs.gnome.org/mclasen/2019/07/27/more-text-rendering-updates/), XeTeX has since 2013,
   with ICU's character properties
   (https://khaledhosny.github.io/2013/03/15/xetex-ported-to-harfbuzz-and-more.html), and so does
   SILE. Prince shapes with its own Allsorts, which copies HarfBuzz's Hebrew order but not the 5.0.0
   rule (https://github.com/yeslogic/allsorts). LuaLaTeX's node mode normalizes to NFC before
   shaping and is reported to mishandle letters with several marks
   (https://github.com/olsak/OpTeX/discussions/158, 2024-08-09); its HarfBuzz mode has been babel's
   default for Hebrew since babel 24.14
   (https://latex3.github.io/babel/news/whats-new-in-babel-24.14.html).
4. **Unicode 18's two new points,** HEBREW POINT SHEVA NA MUDGASH (U+05C8) and HEBREW POINT DAGESH
   HAZAQ MUDGASH (U+05C9), are known to HarfBuzz from 14.5.0, of 2026-09-20, and to ICU from 79.1,
   of 2026-10-22; TeX Live's sources still pin ICU 78.2, so XeTeX treats both as unassigned
   (https://github.com/TeX-Live/texlive-source/blob/e73d75f8e070869316e6a148cfda73ab05abcaf3/libs/README).
   MAM's data holds neither today: the planning session counted none in `MAM-simple/`,
   `MAM-parsed/plus/`, `in/mam-ws-intro/` or `Taamey-D/qa-in/` on 2026-11-22. But MAM-normal order
   provides for U+05C9 (`AGENTS.md`, “Hebrew marks go in MAM-normal order, not Unicode-normal
   order”), so the engine chosen must know both points before MAM uses them.
5. **The candidates**, with versions as of 2026-11-22:

   | Toolchain | Shaping | Right to left | PDF/X | EPUB | Cost and license |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | LuaLaTeX with babel 26.16, HarfBuzz mode | HarfBuzz | mature | X-1a, X-3, X-4 through pdfx | no | free |
   | XeLaTeX with polyglossia and bidi | HarfBuzz, ICU's properties | mature, frozen | through pdfx | no | free |
   | ConTeXt LMTX | its own | capable, thinly documented for Hebrew | its own | not checked | free |
   | WeasyPrint 70.0 | Pango, then HarfBuzz | documented as unsupported | X-1a to X-5g | no | BSD |
   | Prince 16 | Allsorts | supported, limits unchecked | X-1a, X-3, X-4 | no | $495 for the desktop licence |
   | Vivliostyle CLI 11.5.0 | Chromium, then HarfBuzz | Chromium's | X-1a only through a deprecated step | yes | AGPL-3.0 |
   | AH Formatter V7.5 | unknown | Hebrew listed | X-1a, X-3, X-4 | no | $1,250, then $250 a year |

6. **Print.** LuaLaTeX with babel in HarfBuzz mode is the most mature right-to-left path: babel
   26.16 sets columns right to left, has a footnote layout for bidirectional documents, and counts
   in Hebrew letters (https://ctan.math.illinois.edu/macros/latex/required/babel/base/babel.pdf).
   PDF/X comes from the pdfx package, three of whose four maintainers CTAN marks inactive
   (https://ctan.org/pkg/pdfx); LaTeX's own `\DocumentMetadata` writes the metadata and
   validates nothing. LaTeX's team adds no new functionality for XeTeX (LaTeX News 40, November
   2024). Of the HTML-to-PDF engines, Vivliostyle has footnotes, page floats and `direction`, but
   its PDF/X step is deprecated and only labels the file
   (https://github.com/vivliostyle/vivliostyle-cli/blob/main/docs/special-output-settings.md).
   So every route needs a preflight of its PDF/X that does not trust the file's own label.
7. **EPUB.** EPUB 3.3, of 2026-01-13, takes `page-progression-direction="rtl"` on the spine, and
   WOFF2 is one of its core media types; font obfuscation is a last resort that reading systems
   need not decode (https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-33/), and Taamey D is free to copy, so it goes in
   unobfuscated. Apple Books uses a book's embedded fonts only when the book asks it to
   (https://help.apple.com/itc/booksassetguide/en.lproj/itc74d42b31e.html). The most faithful
   EPUB is XHTML that the generator writes itself, with every code point exact, checked by
   EPUBCheck and by DAISY's Ace.
8. **Prior art.** SIL's PTXprint typesets Scripture with footnotes from USFM
   (https://software.sil.org/ptxprint/), and eBible.org distributes the WLC as EPUB 3 and PDF
   (https://ebible.org/details.php?id=hboWLC).
9. **So:** the generator writes XHTML, which is the EPUB; print takes the most mature engine,
   LuaLaTeX in HarfBuzz mode, or the same XHTML through Vivliostyle, if Stage 1 finds that it
   sets MAM as well, which would leave one source for both; and an independent PDF/X preflight
   checks every interior before upload.

### Identifiers

1. **An ISBN identifies one format of one edition.** A multi-volume set takes an ISBN for the set
   and one for each volume; each volume's own ISBN is required when the volumes are sold
   separately, and each volume should list all the set's ISBNs
   (https://www.isbn-international.org/node/88).
2. **A publisher buys from the agency of the country where it is based**
   (https://www.isbn-international.org/node/87), so Ben would buy from Bowker, at $125 for one,
   $295 for 10, $575 for 100 and $1,500 for 1,000. Those prices are unconfirmed: the sub-agent
   could not fetch myidentifiers.com and took them from its search snippets and from guides of
   October 2026 (https://www.myidentifiers.com/get-your-isbn-now). Israel's agency is not the
   National Library of Israel but the Israel Center for Libraries, which charges and addresses
   publishers based in Israel (https://icl.org.il/isbn).
3. **A Kindle edition needs no ISBN;** Amazon gives it an ASIN
   (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G7DMSKCM9DVS65TC).
4. **A service's free ISBN ties the book to that service.** KDP's covers print only, names the
   imprint “Independently published”, and cannot be used elsewhere
   (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G201834170). IngramSpark's, for publishers in the United
   States, names “Indy Pub” and serves one IngramSpark title
   (https://www.ingramspark.com/free-isbn). Lulu's needs its Global Distribution and makes Lulu the
   publisher
   (https://help.lulu.com/en/support/solutions/articles/64000255597-publishing-a-print-book-for-global-distribution).
5. **How many.** One ISBN for each volume in each printed format, and one for the set in each
   format if the set is sold as a unit: five volumes in paperback alone take five, or six with a
   set ISBN, and a hardcover doubles that; the two introductions take one each per format. A
   hundred ISBNs bought once cover the whole plan with room for later editions.

### Rights and the platforms' rules

1. **The license allows selling.** Hebrew Wikisource's text is under CC BY-SA 4.0, and since the
   Wikimedia Terms of Use of 7 June 2023 also under the GFDL, at the reuser's choice; edits made
   before then stay under CC BY-SA 3.0 as well, and the introduction's chapter 1, begun in 2013,
   holds both (https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Policy:Terms_of_Use, section 7;
   https://creativecommons.org/2023/06/29/wikipedia-moves-to-cc-4-0-licenses/). English
   Wikisource's policy allows commercial reuse and treats a translation as derived from its
   source (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Wikisource:Copyright_policy). MAM's own statement puts
   all of MAM under CC BY-SA 4.0 (“Sources in this repository”, item 8).
2. **What CC BY-SA 4.0 asks of a sold book**
   (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en):
   1. credit in the manner the licensor reasonably asks, which for MAM is its own statement's
      attribution; the copyright, license and disclaimer notices kept; a link where practical;
      changes marked; and the license's text or address (section 3(a));
   2. an adaptation licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0 or later (section 3(b)); but a change made only
      to fit a format is never an adaptation (section 2(a)(4)), and Creative Commons' FAQ says
      the same of formatting a work first published online for print, while a translation is an
      adaptation (https://creativecommons.org/faq/);
   3. no added terms and no technical protection that limit what a recipient may do with the
      text (section 2(a)(5)(C)): so **the Kindle edition is sold without DRM**, which KDP leaves to
      the publisher (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GDDXGH9VR22ACM8U);
   4. no suggestion that the licensor endorses the edition (sections 2(a)(6) and 2(b)(2)).
3. **The platforms' rules are the obstacle.**
   1. **KDP** refuses copyrighted content that is freely available on the web “unless it's
      provided by the owner of the copyright”, says nothing of Creative Commons, and may ask for
      proof of rights, which a copyright page does not supply
      (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672390,
      https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672400). A book wholly or mostly in the public
      domain must be set apart by original material, such as annotations or a translation, earns
      only the 35% royalty, and is excluded from KDP Select, which also demands exclusivity that a
      text free on Wikisource cannot give
      (https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200743940,
      https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200644210,
      https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/GD9PMU58BV24QFZ7).
   2. **IngramSpark** refuses content free on the web unless the publisher owns its copyright, and
      may decline public-domain works that differ little from books already sold
      (https://www.ingramspark.com/content-guidelines).
   3. **Lulu's retail channels** reject public-domain content and content free elsewhere, with no
      exception for the owner; they also require titles in Latin letters and take only English
      eBooks
      (https://help.lulu.com/en/support/solutions/articles/64000295259-common-retail-distribution-rejection-reasons).
      Lulu's own store may still sell such a book
      (https://help.lulu.com/en/support/solutions/articles/64000255498).
4. **So the deciding question is whether Avi Kadish takes part.** MAM was “prepared by Seth (Avi)
   Kadish”, its introduction is his to edit (`in/mam-ws-intro/README.md`), and Ben, though a
   contributor to both, is not their only copyright owner. If Avi supplies the content, or gives Ben
   written authorization that a platform accepts, KDP and IngramSpark can take it; if not, the
   editions are limited to channels that take freely licensed content, such as Lulu's own store.
   Whether a platform accepts a licensee under CC BY-SA, or an owner's written permission, as
   content that the owner supplies is unconfirmed: only the platform can say. Ben chose to ask Avi
   before Stage 1 (“Decisions ledger”, entry 5).
5. **Questions for a lawyer, if Ben wants one,** and not answered here: whether Amazon's terms for
   Kindle buyers add terms that section 2(a)(5)(C) forbids even without DRM; whether an edition
   that adds front matter and appendices is a collection or an adaptation; how a notice should
   word text under both CC BY-SA 3.0 and 4.0; and whether KDP would treat MAM as mostly public
   domain, since its consonantal text is ancient while its edition is modern.

## The design

### The three editions

| Edition | Made from | eBook | Printed book |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Hebrew introduction | the pages of `in/mam-ws-intro/` that Stage 3 selects | the generator's EPUB 3, for Kindle and the other stores | the print toolchain that Stage 1 chooses, in one column, right to left |
| The English introduction | the mirror `in/mam-ws-intro-en/`, which `doc/PLAN-english-intro.md` keeps | the same, left to right | the same, left to right |
| The MAM edition | `MAM-parsed/plus/`, text and notes, through the render-element trees; the appendices from `MAM-parsed/plus/` and `in/mam-ws-special/` | the same EPUB 3, one book for each volume; on Kindle only once Stage 1's device test passes | the print toolchain that Stage 1 chooses, at the trim and columns it chooses, right to left |

### The volume map

1. **Spans.** A volume is an ordered list of spans, and a span is a range of verses within one of
   MAM's 39 books, the books as MAM-with-doc's pages count them, from
   `gh-pages/MAM-with-doc/A1-Genesis.html` to `FD-2Chronicles.html`: Samuel, Kings, Chronicles
   and Ezra–Nehemiah are two books each there, and each of the Twelve is one. A span runs from a
   first chapter and verse to a last. So a volume can hold part of a book, one book, or several
   books, and a book can run across several volumes: Ben's one-to-many and many-to-one.
2. **The map is data.** A tracked JSON file, `in/book-editions/mam-volumes.json`, lists the
   volumes in order. For each it gives a number, its titles, its spans, and the appendices it
   carries. The generator reads it, and nothing in the code names a volume's range.
3. **A lint keeps the map whole.** `py/tests/test_book_editions_volume_map.py`, lint-shaped as
   the testing rule asks, checks that the spans run in MAM's book order with no gap and no
   overlap, so that every verse of `MAM-parsed/plus/` lies in exactly one volume, and that each
   span begins at a chapter's first verse and ends at a chapter's last, except where the map
   gives the reason for another boundary, such as a parashah that begins inside a chapter. A
   missing book file fails the lint rather than skipping it.
4. **A page budget bounds each volume.** Each built volume stays under the chosen service's page
   limit for its trim, paper and binding, with room left for its front matter, its appendices
   and later growth. The generator reports each volume's page count, and a build over the limit
   fails.
5. **Breaks fall where readers already find one.** In order of preference: a book's end; a
   division of the book itself, such as the five books of Psalms (1–41, 42–72, 73–89, 90–106 and
   107–150); in the Torah, a parashah's beginning; otherwise a chapter boundary chosen to balance
   the volumes.
6. **The books keep MAM's order:** the Torah; Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings, Isaiah, Jeremiah,
   Ezekiel and the Twelve; Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the Five Megillot (Song of Songs, Ruth,
   Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah and Chronicles. MAM-with-doc's file
   names group them in six, A to F: the Torah, the Former Prophets, the Latter Prophets, Psalms
   to Job, the Megillot, and Daniel to Chronicles.
7. **Ordinary paper.** Ben, 2026-11-22, of the finding that no print-on-demand service offers the
   thin paper Bibles are printed on: “Our multi-volume approach has various benefits, one of
   which is that we can use normal-thickness paper.”
8. **Candidate arrangements.** Page counts are model (a)'s with the notes, each part on its own
   line, from “MAM's size in print, with its documentation notes”, before front matter and
   appendices; a text setting as loose as Koren's adds about a quarter to the text's share.
   1. **Three volumes, Torah, Nevi'im and Ketuvim,** as Breuer's edition for Mossad HaRav Kook
      divided them: about 378, 539 and 497 pages. Nevi'im is the volume nearest a limit: about
      650 pages at Koren's density, over KDP's 550 for a hardcover, which in Hebrew KDP binds
      only left to right anyway, and under 828 for its paperback.
   2. **Five volumes, by MAM's own groups:** the Torah (about 378 pages); the Former Prophets
      (258); the Latter Prophets (282); Psalms, Proverbs and Job (256); and the Five Megillot with
      Daniel to Chronicles (241), since the Megillot alone (61) would make too thin a volume.
   3. **Six volumes:** arrangement 2 with the Torah in two, Genesis and Exodus (175) and
      Leviticus to Deuteronomy (203), which balances all six between about 175 and 282 pages.
   4. **Smaller volumes, by choice.** Any of these can be divided further, down to parts of
      books, if Ben wants lighter volumes: the map allows it, and no page limit forces it.

   Da'at Miqra is the example of the principle, not a candidate: Ben, 2026-11-22, “I was just
   giving it as an example, not at all suggesting (as you perhaps inferred) that we would use its
   exact division.” Its division would give MAM volumes of about 23 to 82 pages (“How printed
   Bibles divide into volumes”, item 5).

   Recommendation: the five volumes of arrangement 2. They follow divisions a reader of the
   Hebrew Bible already knows, each holds whole books, so that no reader looks for the rest of a
   book in another volume, and each is far under every service's limit; arrangement 3 is the
   choice if the Torah's 378 pages should come down to the others' size. Ben chooses at Stage 2,
   when Stage 1's measured pages replace the model's.
9. **Titles and numbering.** Volumes are numbered in MAM's book order, and each volume's title
   names its range, such as a book's name, “Psalms 1–72”, or “the Twelve”. Whether the titles
   and the spine are in Hebrew only, or in Hebrew and English, is deferred to Stage 5.

### What the text shows

1. **What MAM-with-doc shows, unless Ben decides otherwise.** The book is a new consumer of the
   render-element trees that MAM-with-doc's generator renders. Its mapping names every render tag
   that MAM-with-doc's mapping handles, each with its book rendering, and raises on any other
   tag; the same unused-tag survey runs over it. That is the closed dispatch that the user-level
   rule “Template dispatch is closed” requires, and it makes every display choice explicit:
   which of ketiv and qere, which strand in the two Decalogues and in Genesis 35:22, which vowel
   alternative, and which form of a chanted word that has a stress helper. Stage 2 records
   MAM-with-doc's answer to each in the generator's dispatch, and the book follows it.
2. **The documentation notes are printed** (“Decisions ledger”, entry 1). How they are set is
   deferred to Stage 1's trial: as MAM-with-doc sets them, each lemma and each part on a line of
   its own, at the foot of the page or in a column beside the text; or run on as an apparatus;
   and, in an eBook, as notes that open from a mark in the text.
3. **Layout features.** Stage 2 lists every layout feature from the render-tag survey rather than
   from memory. The ones known now are the petuḥah and setumah breaks, the layout of poetic
   verses, the large and small letters, the inverted nuns, MAM's gray maqaf (`MAM-parsed/README.md`,
   “consumer cautions”), and the verse repeated after the last verse of Isaiah, Malachi,
   Ecclesiastes and Lamentations (MAM-simple's `good-ending`).
4. **Numbers.** Chapters and verses are numbered in Hebrew letters, as Hebrew Bibles number them.

### The appendices

1. **The Decalogue appendix** (requirement 5) sets each Decalogue in its תחתון and עליון
   cantillations side by side, in the Tiberian tradition, as the first two sections of
   `in/mam-ws-special/decalogue.mediawiki` do. The main text shows the combined representation,
   as MAM-with-doc does: the render option `ro_cantillation` chooses among `rv-cant-combined`,
   its default, `rv-cant-alef`, `rv-cant-bet` and `rv-cant-all-three`
   (`py/render_wt/render_wikitext.py`, the comment at its top, which counts three passages with
   two cantillations: the Reuben verse and the two Decalogues), and MAM-with-doc's generator sets
   no value, while MAM-simple's sets `rv-cant-all-three` (`py/mb_xml/xml_render.py`). So the
   appendix renders the two Decalogues from `MAM-parsed/plus/` once with `rv-cant-alef` and once
   with `rv-cant-bet`, the data that the main text comes from, and cannot differ from it; its
   arrangement, headings and order come from the Decalogue page.
   Stage 6 first compares those strands with the sections of `decalogue-base.mediawiki` that the
   page transcludes, letter for letter and mark for mark, and reports any difference to Ben
   before choosing a source. Deferred to Stage 6: whether the appendix also carries the printed
   tradition's versions, the page's Exodus-against-Deuteronomy section, its pointed study
   edition, the vowels page, and the Reuben verse, Genesis 35:22, in its
   cantillations פשוטה and מדרשית.
2. **The song-form appendix** (requirement 6, Ben's “maybe”) would set the eight passages' song
   forms, from the `*-layout` pages, perhaps with their further forms from the `*-alternates` pages.
   It follows MAM's own design: MAM's introduction gives the song forms, as it gives the Decalogue's
   two cantillations, pages of their own beside the continuous text of the chapters, for two reasons
   it states. The scribes' customs for the Torah's song form differ slightly, and a page of its own
   can set the important ones side by side for the reader to choose among; and a song form does not
   always suit a reader who wants to read the text continuously, or to copy it. It does the same for
   two full performances of a form like a song's, the ten sons of Haman and the song of the times
   (`in/mam-ws-intro/ch2.mediawiki`, the subsection headed צורת השיר במהדורתנו, line 730, and the
   list of song-form pages after it). So the MAM edition's main text, like MAM's chapters, runs on
   through those passages, and a song form appears only in the appendix. A song form is a fixed
   arrangement of text and gaps on the page. A printed page keeps it exactly; a reflowable eBook
   cannot, since the reader's font size and screen width move every line. Recommendation, deferred
   to Stage 6: include it in the printed MAM edition, and in the Kindle edition only if Stage 1's
   trial of one song-form page shows a form that Kindle keeps intact. Kindle's only fixed-layout
   fallback draws text as images (“Kindle”, item 6), so a song form that reflowable text cannot keep
   would reach Kindle only as an image, or not at all.
3. **Where the appendices go** is deferred to Stage 6: at the end of the volume that holds each
   passage, so that Exodus 20's Decalogue and the Song of the Sea sit in the volume with Exodus;
   or all together in the last volume; or in a volume of their own.

### Typography and layout

1. **Pointed Hebrew is set in Taamey D**, as the user-level instructions require, embedded in
   every PDF and EPUB. Software that draws the text takes the font from this repository's
   `Taamey-D/font-m-TaD/Taamey_D.ttf` at the pinned release, never from a copy installed on the
   machine.
2. **Unpointed Hebrew keeps the surrounding font.** The Hebrew introduction's prose, the headings
   and the running heads need a Hebrew text face whose license permits embedding; English prose
   needs a Latin one. Both choices are deferred to Stage 1, which tries candidates.
3. **Trim, columns and margins** come from Stage 1's measurements against the chosen service's
   limits. The two page models of “MAM's size in print, with its documentation notes” are the
   starting candidates: 6 × 9 inches in one column, or 7 × 10 in two.
4. **Running heads** name the book and chapter on each page of the MAM edition, and the chapter
   on each page of an introduction.

## Risks

1. **Kindle's rendering of cantillation.** No source shows that any Kindle renderer positions Hebrew
   marks from an embedded font, and a reader can turn the publisher's font off (“Kindle”, item 5),
   so the Kindle edition of anything with cantillation waits on Stage 1's device test.
2. **The platforms' rules on freely available content,** which Ben's request to Avi Kadish before
   Stage 1 addresses (“Decisions ledger”, entry 5).
3. **Right-to-left binding:** a right-to-left hardcover exists only at IngramSpark, and KDP's
   Hebrew paperback sells only on Amazon.
4. **The text moves.** MAM and its introduction change on Wikisource, so each printing is a
   snapshot whose corrections wait for the next printing (Stage 8).
5. **Fragile mark orders in the introduction,** which is exempt from the MAM-normal rule: Stage 3
   reads them and reports any to Ben, never rewriting the mirror.
6. **The font's license in a sold eBook.** The font-embedding exception keeps the document free
   of the GPL, but the font inside an EPUB is still a copy of a GPL version 2 program, so each
   edition's copyright page names the font, its license, its copyright holders and the address
   of the release's source archive under `gh-pages/taamey-d/downloads/`. Whether that suffices
   is Ben's judgment as the font's maintainer, or a lawyer's.
7. **Programs installed on Ben's machine,** each on his word (Stage 1).
