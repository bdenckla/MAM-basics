# The Feldheim Simanim Tanakh's sixteen signs

The Simanim Tanakh (מכון סימנים, Jerusalem; Feldheim, תשפ"ג/2023; edited by Shmuel Meir Riachi,
who also cut its fonts) prints an ordinary Masoretic text in a typeface designed to carry a
second layer of information on top of it. Letters are stretched or hollowed, vowels and accents
are redrawn, thickened, enlarged, doubled or emptied out, and marginalia carry dates and topic
headings. None of this is Masoretic notation. It is a designed reading aid, and the edition
documents the whole of it in its own front matter: sixteen numbered *ma'alot* over pp. יא-כז
(scans `2-11`…`2-27`), preceded by a publisher's preface on pp. ט-י.

This doc is an index of those sixteen. It exists because two sessions have now found a mark on
the page, treated it as an open question, and only afterwards discovered the edition had
answered it in print. The edition says as much itself: a boxed line on the page facing the
preface (scan `2-08`) reads **קריאת המבוא הכרחית להבנת ה"סימנים" בתוך התנ"ך** — reading the
introduction is essential to understanding the signs inside the Tanakh. Read this before
transcribing from the volume; [#72](https://github.com/bdenckla/wlc-utils/issues/72) has three
more of its Decalogues to do (pp. 297-298, and the *elyon* pair the table of contents places at
p. 349).

**This is a description, not a translation.** Hebrew below is quoted only where a term is the
thing being named.

## What can be mistaken for an accent

Four things, and they are the reason to read this page at all.

**The rafe (ma'ala 9) is not an accent.** A short zigzag stroke above a letter, sitting higher
than any accent does, is this edition's rafe, restored where nearly every other printed Tanakh
dropped it. It appears only in four listed situations, all of them consonantal rather than
musical, so it never competes with an accent for its slot — but it does sit in the accent zone,
and it is easy to read as one. It is not transcribed.

**The stroke after a word is hollow or solid, and the difference is the point (ma'ala 16).** The
one vertical line can be *munax-legarmeh*, an accent and a light disjunctive, or the narrow-sense
paseq, which is no accent at all but a reading aid on the order of maqaf. This edition prints the
narrow-sense paseq **hollow** and leaves the **solid** bar to legarmeh, throughout the volume. So
`pasoleg_kinds: distinguished` is documentary for this edition rather than inferred, and a stroke
must be looked at, not merely noticed.

**A doubled accent is one accent (ma'ala 11).** Zarqa, segolta, telisha gedola and telisha
qetana sit at a fixed edge of the word rather than on its stressed syllable; this edition prints
a second copy of the glyph on the stress. Two glyphs, one accent, one token. Counting the copy
would inflate a transcription's token count against the reference stream.

**A small ring above a letter is a chronology marker (ma'ala 4).** The edition marks the point in
the text where a datable event occurs with a hollow circle set at accent height, with the year in
the margin opposite. On Joshua 1:1-2 as the introduction itself prints it (p. טו) the ring stands
above the mem of מֹשֶׁה, clear of the holam dot and looking very like a Masoretic circellus. It
is neither that nor an accent.

## The sixteen ma'alot

The introduction groups them under four section headings, which run as the page headers and are
the fastest way to tell where you are.

### נסח ועריכה — text and editing (ma'alot 1-4, pp. יא-טו)

**1. הנוסח** (p. יא). The base text is the Aleppo Codex, with the Leningrad manuscript filling
places the Aleppo lacks, and R. Shalom Shachna Yellin's lists supplying petuxot and setumot where
it is defective; Esther keeps the customary section divisions rather than the Aleppo order, to
minimize dispute. The ma'ala also covers page layout — the shape of the שירות and the setting of
the אמ"ת books in two columns, both taken from the Aleppo manuscript — and the line-breaking
devices that follow from it, including gray maqafs used to fill a line where a blank is wanted.
The preface (p. י) is blunt that none of this is meant to decide halakhic questions of petuxot,
setumot or ketiv/qere; the text was chosen to serve correct reading, not to settle sofrut.

**2. קרי וכתיב** (pp. יב-יג). The qere is set in the text column itself rather than banished to
the margin, with the ketiv beside it in gray, unpointed, at reduced size. כתיב ולא קרי, קרי ולא
כתיב, and the words the masorah counts as נקודים each get their own treatment. A ketiv/qere pair
spanning two or three words is set as a single unit rather than word by word.

**3. מאורעות התנ"ך** (pp. יג-יד). A running list of events appears as a side-heading (כותרת צד)
in the outer margin, pointed, naming what happens in the lines beside it. A subject index at the
back lists every such heading with its page. The introduction is careful that these are chapter
headings for the general subject, not a summary of every detail in the passage.

**4. סדר הדורות** (pp. יד-טו). Three devices for chronology: the year of an event in the margin
opposite its line, tied to the text by the hollow circle described above; the span of each book's
events on its title page; and colour chronological tables at the back running from Adam to the
end of the Tanakh period. Dates are anno mundi and generally follow the well-known reckoning; an
approximate date carries a geresh (`[3316']`) and a span is written as a range (`[2920-23]`).

### סימנים באותיות ובניקוד — signs in the letters and the pointing (ma'alot 5-9, pp. טז-כב)

**5. אותיות** (pp. טז-יט). The largest ma'ala, and a font design rather than a single sign: a
medium-weight letter cut with room inside it for two distinct dageshes, from which several
letter-level signs are derived. A letter whose pronunciation is at stake is drawn **stretched**
(אות משוכה) — mappiq he, as in Genesis 2:15 לְעָבְדָהּ וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ, and alef/he/ayin bearing a
shva inside a word, whose colour follows the preceding vowel. Three more follow: **פתח גנובה**,
where a final ayin, xet or he pointed with patax has the patax shifted to the letter's right side
as early scribes wrote it; a sin distinguished from a shin by the height of the shin's left leg,
lest קמשונים and the like be misread; and the quiescent alef, written but unpronounced, drawn as a
**hollow** outline letter (אות חלולה).

**6. דגש חזק** (p. יט). Every letter carrying a dagesh xazak is set in a thickened form, so that
strong and weak dagesh are distinguishable at a glance rather than by rule. This is the payoff of
the ma'ala-5 font work, which reserved room inside the letter for exactly this contrast.

**7. שוא נע** (pp. כ-כא). Shva na is printed **כשני ריבועים מוגדלים** — as two enlarged squares —
against shva nax, which keeps its ordinary form of two small round dots. The contrast is plainly
visible in the edition's own example, Joshua 1:3 כַּף־רַגְלְכֶם, where the shva under the gimel
is round and small and the one under the lamed is square and large. The edition follows the
common system, broadly that of *Minxat Shai*; the Aramaic chapters of Daniel and Ezra, where no
consistent masorah was found, follow the Yemenite reading tradition instead.

**8. קמץ קטן** (p. כא). Kamatz katan gets a new glyph — a kamatz whose downstroke is pulled
downward — distinguishing it from ordinary broad kamatz, with xataf kamatz treated to match.
This is one of the three emphases R. Ovadia Yosef's approbation singles out, alongside shva na
and dagesh xazak.

**9. סימן הרפה** (pp. כא-כב). The rafe, dropped from every existing printed Tanakh, is restored
here **partially**, in four situations: on a word-initial bet/gimel/dalet/kaf/pe/tav after a word
ending in alef, he, vav or yod; on the same letters after a shva na, the masorah's רפי; after the
he of a question, and there even on letters outside that set; and anywhere a misreading is
likely. Its worked example is Joshua 1:2 וכל־העם. Note that the edition files the rafe with the
pointing, not with the accents — it is the last ma'ala under this section heading.

### סימנים והדגשות בטעמים — signs and emphases in the accents (ma'alot 10-12, pp. כג-כד)

**10. גודל הטעמים** (p. כג). The accent glyphs are newly drawn and enlarged to roughly half a
letter's height. This is why accents on the page are legible enough to transcribe at all, and
why a crop that clips a millimetre of the line above or below loses real information.

**11. הטעמה כפולה** (pp. כג-כד). The four accents that sit at a fixed edge of the word rather
than on its stressed syllable — zarqa, segolta, telisha gedola, telisha qetana — are printed
twice, the extra copy marking the stress. Illustrated with Judges 18:19 ויאמרו לו החרש. The
following case, נסוג אחור, is handled the same way. **Both glyphs together are one accent.**

**12. מתגים** (p. כד). Metegs the manuscripts omit are supplied, particularly before a shva that
is to be sounded and before xatafs. In the אמ"ת books a **מתג מושאל**, a borrowed meteg, marks the
stress on words accented with *dexi*, whose own glyph stands before the word and so cannot show
where the stress falls.

### הבדלים בין טעמים דומים — distinctions among similar accents (ma'alot 13-16, pp. כה-כז)

**13. קדמא ופשטא** (p. כה). Two accents of near-identical shape and opposite function. Pashta is
printed **thicker**, expressing something of the strength of its pause, against the lighter qadma;
the example is Genesis 22:17 והרבה ארבה את־זרעך. The ma'ala also changes where תרי־פשטין is
placed: both marks now sit at the end of the word, as in the old manuscripts and as *Minxat Shai*
notes, rather than the first being set mid-word as printings had come to do.

**14. מהפך ויתיב** (pp. כה-כו). The same problem for the servus mahapakh against the disjunctive
yetiv: yetiv is drawn **sharpened and drawn out** from the mahapakh, a distinction the
introduction notes was already made in some Xumashim. The example is Genesis 22:16 כי יען אשר
עשית. Being prepositive, yetiv also stands at the start of its word, where mahapakh sits under
the stressed letter — but shape, not position, is what this ma'ala fixes.

**15. טרחא ודחי** (p. כו). In the אמ"ת books, the servus *tarxa* sits under the stressed letter
while the disjunctive *dexi* stands before the word, at its start; the two are otherwise the same
glyph. Two further devices show whether the word is מלעיל or מלרע, the borrowed meteg of ma'ala
12 among them.

**16. מונח־לגרמיה** (p. כז). The last, and the one with the most direct bearing on transcription.
The single vertical stroke is either the accent *munax-legarmeh*, which is in the list of accents
and is one of the light disjunctives, or its non-accent fellow, which is not in that list and is a
reading aid like maqaf — a slight pause between two words, with no melody of its own. Since the
Masoretic פסיקתא lists that record the second are not available to every reader, the edition
prints the narrow-sense paseq as a **hollow** bar throughout the volume and leaves the solid bar
to legarmeh, with the two glyphs set side by side and worked examples from 1 Kings 7:25.

The page after the last ma'ala (p. כח, scan `2-28`) is not a ma'ala but the accent-name tables,
Ashkenazi and Sephardi, split into disjunctives and servi — with the footnote that פסק, מקף and
מתג are **not** in the list of accents at all.

## The display-font trap

The introduction draws its own signs in a display font, and that font need not match the body
font. The rafe is the demonstrated case: the introduction sets it as a **solid horizontal bar**,
including in the large Joshua 1:1-2 specimen on p. טו, while the body text at Joshua 1:2 (p. 357)
has a **zigzag** over the kaf of that same word.

So match a sign by its **example word**, not by its shape. Every ma'ala gives at least one, and
the example is a verse reference you can look up in the body of the same volume and compare. A
sign identified from the introduction's drawing alone may not be the thing you are looking at.
